How Many Sessions of CBT Does It Take to Treat Depression?

October 16, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: A standard course of CBT for depression is about 8 sessions for milder depression and about 16 sessions for more severe depression, according to the UK's NICE depression guideline (NG222). Some people need more, mainly when they have other health problems or symptoms that linger. Many people notice change within the first several weeks. If nothing has shifted after 4 to 6 weeks, that is a signal to review the plan.

What do treatment guidelines say about the number of CBT sessions?

Most published guidance does not give one fixed number. The clearest numbers come from the NICE guideline for adults, updated in 2022. It sets a usual course length for each type of therapy and splits depression into "less severe" and "more severe."

  • Individual CBT, less severe depression: usually 8 regular sessions.
  • Group CBT, less severe depression: usually 8 regular sessions, with about 8 people in the group and 2 practitioners.
  • Guided self-help based on CBT: usually 6 to 8 structured support sessions while you work through printed or digital materials.
  • Individual CBT, more severe depression: usually 16 regular sessions, alone or combined with an antidepressant.

NICE adds that extra sessions may be needed for people with other mental or physical health conditions, complex social needs, or residual symptoms. So 8 or 16 is a starting point, not a cap. If you are not sure which approach fits you, our guide to therapy types for depression compares CBT with the main alternatives.

Do more sessions always work better?

Not in a straight line. A 2013 metaregression in the Journal of Affective Disorders pooled 70 studies with 5,403 patients who got individual therapy for depression. It found only a small link between the number of sessions and the size of the benefit. That link was no longer significant once the authors adjusted for other study features. Total contact time and total length of therapy did not predict outcomes either.

A 2020 systematic review of 26 studies in routine care described a curved pattern. Gains come fastest early on and then level off. The review put the best "dose" in routine settings anywhere from 4 to 26 sessions, and 4 to 6 for low-intensity guided self-help. The right number varied by setting, by patient group, and by how outcomes were measured.

The practical point: there is no magic number. A plan that fits your severity, and then gets checked against your progress, matters more than hitting a session count.

Does it matter how often you go?

It may matter more than the total. In the same 2013 analysis, going from one to two sessions a week raised the effect size by g = 0.45 while the total number of sessions stayed the same. The authors called for more research before treating this as settled. The 2020 review also found that weekly therapy seemed to speed up improvement compared with less frequent schedules.

If your schedule allows it, regular weekly sessions are a reasonable default.

When do most people start to feel better in CBT?

Change often shows up in the first part of treatment. Some people have a "sudden gain," a large drop in symptoms between two sessions. A meta-analysis of 16 studies with 1,104 participants found that people with sudden gains had better results at the end of treatment and at follow-up. The link was stronger in CBT than in other therapies.

Not everyone improves in one jump. Gradual progress is common and still counts. What should prompt a talk with your therapist is no change at all. NICE advises reviewing the plan if depression has not responded at all after 4 to 6 weeks of psychological therapy. That review looks at missed sessions, outside stressors, other conditions, and whether the diagnosis is right. If you reach that point, our article on what to do when therapy is not helping covers the next steps.

Depression and anxiety often occur together. When they do, a therapist may add sessions or target both. Our sister site anxiety.md covers CBT for anxiety in more depth.

Do you need booster sessions after CBT ends?

Many people do well with a few extra sessions after the main course. CBT appears to have effects that outlast treatment. In a 2005 trial of moderate to severe depression, people who responded to cognitive therapy could have no more than 3 booster sessions over the next year. Their relapse rate was 30.8%, compared with 76.2% for people switched from medication to placebo. It was not statistically different from the 47.2% rate in people who stayed on medication.

Relapse is still common. A meta-analysis of 28 studies with 1,880 adults found that 29% of people who responded to CBT relapsed within 1 year after it stopped, and 54% within 2 years. Continuing CBT past the main course reduced relapse by 21% compared with monitoring alone at the end of continuation treatment.

NICE reflects this. For people at higher risk of relapse who want to keep going, it recommends at least 4 more sessions focused on staying well. For relapse prevention with group CBT or mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, a course is usually 8 sessions over 2 to 3 months, with the option of more sessions over the next 12 months.

Can shorter or online CBT work?

Yes, for many people. A 2021 individual patient data analysis in JAMA Psychiatry drew on 39 trials and 9,751 participants. Both guided and unguided internet CBT beat control conditions in the short and long term. Guided programs, where a coach or therapist checks in, did better at the end of treatment. The edge for guided programs was larger for people with moderate to severe symptoms (PHQ-9 above 9). For mild symptoms, unguided programs worked about as well.

CBT can also help when antidepressants alone have not been enough. In the CoBalT trial, 469 primary care patients who had been on antidepressants for at least 6 weeks were randomized. At 6 months, 46% of those who added CBT responded, compared with 22% on usual care alone.

Skills practice between sessions is part of what makes short courses work. CBT, as the National Institute of Mental Health describes it, teaches you to notice and question harmful automatic thoughts. That work happens mostly outside the room. NICE also lists structured behavioral activation as an option within guided self-help. Our guide to behavioral activation explains how it works.

The bottom line

Expect about 8 weekly sessions of CBT for milder depression and about 16 for more severe depression. Plan for more if other conditions or stressors are in the picture. Session count matters less than regular attendance, practice between sessions, and an honest check on progress around weeks 4 to 6. After you improve, a few booster or relapse prevention sessions can help you stay well.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a full course of CBT for depression take in weeks?

With weekly sessions, an 8-session course takes about 2 months and a 16-session course takes about 4 months. Some people have two sessions a week early on, which shortens the timeline. Relapse prevention sessions can extend contact over the following year.

What happens if I miss CBT sessions?

Missed sessions are one of the first things NICE says to check when therapy is not working. Gaps can slow progress, and weekly attendance appears to speed improvement. Tell your therapist if scheduling is a barrier so the plan can be adjusted.

Can I stop CBT early if I feel better?

You can, but talk it through with your therapist first. Ending sessions often cover relapse warning signs and a plan to stay well. Research links continued or booster CBT with lower relapse rates.

Is 6 sessions of CBT enough for depression?

It can be for milder depression, especially guided self-help, where NICE lists 6 to 8 support sessions. Routine care research puts helpful doses anywhere from 4 to 26 sessions. More severe depression usually needs a longer course.

Does insurance limit the number of CBT sessions?

Some plans cap visits or require prior approval after a set number. Coverage varies widely by plan and country. Ask your insurer how many sessions are covered and what documentation extends them.
