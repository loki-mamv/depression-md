Does ECT Cause Permanent Memory Loss?

October 18, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: For most people, the memory problems from ECT are temporary. A 2010 meta-analysis of 84 studies found that thinking and memory scores dropped in the first 3 days after treatment, then returned to baseline within about two weeks. But some people do lose memories of events from the weeks or months around their treatment, and some of those gaps may never fully return. The risk depends a lot on how ECT is given.

What is ECT like today?

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) uses a brief electric current to trigger a short seizure in the brain while the person is under general anesthesia. According to the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH), the patient first gets a short-acting anesthetic and an intravenous muscle relaxant, so they do not move or feel the pulses. The seizure lasts under a minute. Most people wake 5 to 10 minutes later and feel alert after about an hour.

A typical course is three sessions a week until symptoms improve, usually within 6 to 12 treatments. The FDA has cleared ECT for severe depressive episodes in people 13 and older with major depression or bipolar disorder. It is usually offered when other treatments have failed or when a fast response is needed, for example when someone is suicidal, catatonic, or not eating. If you have tried several medications without relief, our guide to treatment-resistant depression explains where ECT fits among the options.

What kinds of memory problems can ECT cause?

Doctors separate ECT's effects on memory into a few types:

  • Short-term confusion. Right after a session, many people feel groggy or disoriented. This usually clears within hours.
  • Anterograde amnesia. This is trouble forming new memories during the treatment course. You may not remember conversations or visits from those weeks.
  • Retrograde amnesia. This is loss of memories formed before treatment. It mostly affects events from the weeks or months just before and during the course.

NIMH lists memory loss and confusion among the most common side effects. It notes that memory problems are sometimes more severe, but usually improve in the days and weeks after the course ends. Retrograde loss of personal memories is the type behind most reports of lasting harm.

Do memory and thinking recover after ECT?

On standard tests, yes for most people. A 2010 meta-analysis in Biological Psychiatry pooled 84 studies with 2,981 patients. Scores fell on 72% of the measured thinking skills in the first 0 to 3 days after ECT. By 4 to 15 days, almost no skill was still clearly below baseline. After 15 days, no skill was worse than before treatment, and processing speed, working memory, and new learning were often better than at baseline.

Part of that gain likely reflects recovery from depression itself, which slows thinking. Depression's effect on focus and memory is covered in our article on depression brain fog.

There is an important gap. The same meta-analysis found no standardized tests of retrograde amnesia among the studies it reviewed. So its reassuring results apply to attention, learning, and new memory, not to the loss of older personal memories.

Can ECT erase memories permanently?

It can for some people. The best large study is a 2007 study of 347 patients at seven New York area hospitals. Researchers measured personal memories with the Columbia Autobiographical Memory Interview, which asks about specific life events and compares answers before and after treatment. They still found adverse cognitive effects 6 months after the ECT course. Bilateral ECT caused more severe and longer-lasting retrograde amnesia than right unilateral ECT. Older age, lower prior intellectual function, and female sex were linked to larger deficits.

Patient reports suggest the problem is not rare. A 2003 systematic review in the BMJ looked at studies of patients' own views. It concluded that at least one third of patients reported persistent memory loss. The authors found that the studies varied widely in their methods. They challenged the claim that memory loss after ECT is not clinically important.

Measurement is the hard part. Personal memories are hard to test because no one has a full record of what a person knew before treatment. Depression can also blur memories of the illness period. That is why objective tests and patient reports do not always agree.

Concerns about dementia have not held up in large data sets. A Danish study of 168,015 patients with mood disorders found ECT was not linked to a higher risk of later dementia once the researchers accounted for patient selection and deaths.

Does the way ECT is given change the risk?

Yes, and this is the most useful thing to ask about. Two settings matter most:

  • Electrode placement. Bilateral ECT places electrodes on both sides of the head. Right unilateral ECT places one on the right side and one on top. NIMH notes that the right side is opposite the brain's learning and memory areas, and that unilateral ECT carries a lower risk of memory loss.
  • Pulse width. Modern devices use brief or ultrabrief pulses instead of older sine wave stimulation. The 2007 study linked sine wave stimulation to slower reaction times even 6 months later.

There is a tradeoff. A 2015 meta-analysis of six trials with 689 patients compared brief and ultrabrief pulse right unilateral ECT. Ultrabrief pulse caused fewer cognitive side effects in every area tested, including memory of past events. But it was slightly less effective, had lower remission rates, and needed about one more session on average. The authors advised weighing these goals for each patient.

What does the FDA say about ECT and memory?

In December 2018, the FDA placed ECT devices for severe depression in Class II, with special controls. The federal rule (21 CFR 882.5940) requires device labeling to carry this warning: "ECT device use may be associated with: disorientation, confusion, and memory problems." Labeling must also recommend testing memory, attention, and other thinking skills before and during the course.

The rule adds a second warning unless data show longer-term benefit: "The long-term safety and effectiveness of ECT treatment has not been demonstrated." In practice, this means you should expect your care team to check your memory as treatment goes on.

How does the memory risk compare to untreated severe depression?

ECT is used for the most dangerous forms of depression, so the comparison is not ECT versus no risk. A 2022 Canadian study of 67,327 psychiatric hospital stays for depression found that ECT was linked to about half the risk of suicide death in the year after discharge. It was also linked to lower all-cause mortality.

ECT is also used for severe depressive episodes in bipolar disorder. bipolar.md covers that side of treatment. If you are weighing ECT, ask the team which placement and pulse width they plan to use. Ask how they will track your memory, and whether they can adjust if problems appear. It can also help to keep notes, photos, or a journal of key events before treatment starts.

The bottom line

For most people, ECT does not cause permanent memory loss on standard tests, and thinking skills recover within about two weeks. But a meaningful share of people report lasting gaps in personal memories from around the treatment period. Bilateral placement and older stimulation methods raise that risk. Right unilateral, brief or ultrabrief pulse ECT lowers it. For severe or life-threatening depression, many people decide the benefit is worth it after a frank talk with their doctor.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does confusion last after an ECT session?

Most people wake 5 to 10 minutes after a session and feel groggy at first. NIMH says people are usually alert after about an hour. Some people feel more confused as a course goes on, and this usually improves in the days and weeks after the course ends.

Will I forget my whole past after ECT?

No. Memory loss from ECT mostly affects events in the weeks or months around treatment, not a person's whole life history. Some people do report lasting gaps in personal memories from that period, especially after bilateral ECT.

Is right unilateral ECT as effective as bilateral ECT?

Right unilateral ECT is widely used because it causes less memory loss. Some studies find it works slightly less well or needs more sessions, depending on the dose and pulse width. Your psychiatrist can explain which method fits your situation and whether they would switch if you do not improve.

Does ECT cause brain damage or dementia?

A large Danish registry study of 168,015 people with mood disorders found that ECT was not linked to a higher risk of later dementia after the researchers accounted for patient selection and deaths. This does not rule out lasting memory gaps for individuals, which some patients report.

Can I do anything to protect my memories before ECT?

Some people write down key events, save photos, or keep a journal before and during treatment. Ask the team to test your memory before and during the course, as FDA labeling recommends. Tell them right away if you notice memory problems so they can consider changing the technique.
